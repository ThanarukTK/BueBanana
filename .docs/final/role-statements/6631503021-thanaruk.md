## Statement (≈200 words)

I wrote the documents that define the problem and shape the solution: the proposal, the product backlog with
requirements `PB-01…PB-15` and their acceptance criteria, the compliance rules that constrain the build (PDPA,
Computer Crime Act §26, Electronic Transactions Act §9) mapped onto features, and the design draft with the use
case, activity, sequence and class diagrams. I also own the team's repository on GitHub.

My decision was to split check-in and check-out into separate requirements for the group cases rather than
leave them as one story. "Check in with an NFC tag" hides three different behaviours — open a new session, join
the group whose window is still open, or reopen an existing roster — and each one fails differently. Splitting
them into `PB-11`, `PB-12` and `PB-13` is why the test plan can trace a case to a specific group flow instead
of a vague one.

What I would do differently: I wrote the three pains without evidence behind them. They are now sourced in
`docs/planning/secondary-review.md` and in the owner conversation, but that should have happened before the
backlog was written, not after. Related: `PB-14` asks the staff view to name a table, and the data model never
had table identity — a requirement I wrote that the build could not satisfy.

Evidence: `docs/planning/backlog.md`, `docs/compliance/rule.md`, `docs/design/designdraft.md` §4.1–4.4.

## Reflection

Building this app with AI taught me that software engineering depends on asking the right questions before producing an answer. AI helped me turn rough ideas into clearer requirements, acceptance criteria, compliance summaries, and design diagrams more quickly. For example, separating the NFC group flow into PB-11, PB-12, and PB-13 made each behaviour easier for the team to understand, implement, and test. The main improvement was speed: we could move from a discussion to a shared, testable description without spending as much time on the first draft. This also helped teamwork because developers and testers could refer to the same requirement when discussing a problem.

The downside was that a polished AI-assisted document could make an unverified assumption look settled. I wrote the three user pains before collecting enough evidence, and PB-14 required the staff view to show a table name that our data model could not provide. These were requirements and design problems that would carry into coding if nobody challenged them. AI could draft test ideas, but it could not observe real users or decide whether the app was ready to release. It also could not take responsibility for the accuracy of a legal summary or for security risks. As test lead, I had to check sources, connect requirements to test cases, triage failures with the team, and judge the exit criteria using evidence. I learned that faster drafting only improves quality when someone verifies the result.

In the software industry today, I see myself as a developing engineer with a useful starting point, not as someone whose judgment can be replaced by AI. My strongest contributions in this project were analysing requirements, making work traceable, and thinking about how to test the result. I still need to improve at interviewing users, reviewing a running system, and understanding implementation trade-offs. In the AI era, I think my value will come from connecting what people need to what the team builds, then checking whether the product really works and is safe to use.

Next year, I plan to practise gathering evidence before writing requirements, contribute more directly to implementation, and improve my tests for group flows and security risks. I also want to get better at explaining defects clearly so the team can fix them sooner. In five years, I hope to lead quality and design decisions for products people actually use. I want to be the kind of engineer who uses AI confidently, checks its output carefully, and helps a team make decisions it can defend.
