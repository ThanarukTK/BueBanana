## Statement (≈200 words)

I built the frontend: the app layout and landing page, the staff console screen, the login session route
that reports who is signed in, and the customer-facing catalogue page for the 3D-printed products, together
with the app's dependencies and environment template.

My decision was to keep credential checking on the server rather than in the browser. Because the backend has
no staff-account API yet, the console verifies the credentials itself inside the route handler and issues a
signed session cookie, so the password never travels to the client and the staff pages can check a session
instead of trusting a flag the browser sets.

What I would do differently: I restyled the console several times — layout, then UI, then the bottom bar —
because I started from how it should look instead of how it is used. The floor view is read at a counter on a
phone while a customer waits, so the questions to settle first are "what does the staff member need in two
seconds" and "what fits on this screen", and the styling follows. I would sketch that workflow before writing
any CSS.

Evidence: `frontend/app/layout.tsx`, `frontend/app/api/auth/me/route.ts`, `frontend/app/admin/staff/page.tsx`,
`frontend/app/customer/page.tsx`.

## Reflection

Building the frontend with AI made the first version of the app much faster to produce. Codex helped draft layouts, reusable components, styling, and route handlers, so I could show the team a staff floor view and customer catalogue early. That helped us discuss requirements through actual screens: what staff needed during check-in and check-out, and what customers should see in the catalogue. I could then connect the staff page to the test cases instead of judging it only by appearance. The main improvement was speed from an idea to something the team could use and test.

The part that got worse was my design process. Because AI could generate and restyle pages quickly, I spent time changing the console's layout, interface, and bottom bar before I had settled the staff workflow. At a counter, a staff member may have only a few seconds and a phone screen to find the right session. A cleaner visual design does not solve that problem unless the important information appears first. I also learned that working code needs checks beyond the screen. The login route handles credentials, so I rewrote the check to run on the server and checked the session flow rather than accepting the first generated handler. The floor view also showed old demo sessions when I checked the deployment. The price cap still held, but the long displayed duration could confuse someone using the console. These examples showed me how usability, security, data quality, and testing affect the same frontend experience. Team feedback and the test plan helped expose issues that styling alone would not reveal.

Today, I see myself as a frontend developer who can turn a requirement into a working page and use AI to explore options quickly. My responsibility is to understand the generated code, test the behaviour, and notice when the interface does not fit the user's situation. I still need more experience with real-user feedback, small-screen workflows, authentication, and edge cases. Those skills will matter even if AI makes routine coding faster.

Next year, I plan to sketch the staff task before writing CSS, test important flows on a phone early, and strengthen my frontend and authentication tests. I also want to coordinate earlier with backend and testing teammates so the interface displays data that is current and meaningful. In five years, I hope to lead frontend work on reliable products, mentor other developers, and make design decisions based on how people actually use the software.
